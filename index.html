Volunteer mileage & out-of-pocket costs

Your time is never deductible, but the money you spend to give it usually is. Log each trip or expense and the checker on the right tells you where it stands.

Log a trip or expense

Mileage at the statutory 14¢ rate, plus parking, tolls, supplies, travel and more.

What counts

  • ✓
    Driving for the charity

    14¢ per mile (or actual gas and oil), plus parking and tolls. Delivering meals, driving to your shift, hauling donations, transporting clients.

  • ✓
    Supplies you buy and don't get reimbursed for

    Stamps, printing, ingredients, materials, phone calls made for the charity.

  • ✓
    Travel away from home on charity duty

    Air, rail, bus, taxi, lodging and meals when you're on substantial duty throughout the trip. Serving as a delegate at a convention counts.

  • ✓
    Uniforms with no everyday use

    The required shirt, smock or vest, plus the cost of cleaning it.

  • ?
    Travel that mixes duty and pleasure

    Not deductible if there's a significant element of personal pleasure, recreation or vacation. A day or two of sightseeing on a week-long build trip is usually fine; a vacation with a morning of volunteering is not.

What doesn't

  • ✕
    The value of your time or services

    Even a professional donating skilled work can't deduct their hourly rate.

  • ✕
    Local meals during a shift

    Lunch while volunteering in town is personal. Meals only count while away from home overnight.

  • ✕
    Childcare so you can volunteer

    Personal expense, not a gift to the charity.

  • ✕
    Car repairs, insurance, depreciation, registration

    Only gas & oil (or the 14¢ rate) plus parking and tolls.

  • ✕
    Costs of family or friends who tag along

    Only your own share, and only if you're the one performing services.

  • ✕
    Raffle tickets, bingo, lottery

    Games of chance are never charitable contributions.

Fair market value guide

“Fair market value” (FMV) is the price a willing buyer would pay a willing seller for the item in its current condition. For used goods, that's thrift-store money, not what you paid. Pick an item to drop it straight into a goods donation.

How to value donated goods

1. Start from the range below (based on the valuation guides charities publish). 2. Slide toward the low end for age and wear, the high end for like-new, name-brand or in-demand items. 3. Note the method you used; the IRS asks for it on Form 8283. Condition rule: clothing and household items must be in good used condition or better. Worn-out, stained or broken items are worth $0 for tax purposes, no matter what the guide says. Ranges reviewed September 2026 against the Salvation Army Donation Value Guide and regional Goodwill guides; method per IRS Publication 561. Picking an item uses the good-condition value; change the condition on the item row to move it.

When a range isn't enough

Over $500 in goods for the year: Form 8283 Section A. Record when and how you got the items and what you paid. Any single item (or group of similar items) over $5,000: a qualified appraisal is required, plus Form 8283 Section B. Art, jewelry, collectibles and vehicles have special rules (IRS Publication 561). Vehicles over $500: deduction is usually limited to what the charity sells it for; you'll get Form 1098-C.

Rules that shape the number (tax year 2026)

  • ✓
    Don't itemize? You can still deduct cash gifts.

    Starting with 2026 returns, non-itemizers may deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts ($2,000 married filing jointly) to public charities, on top of the standard deduction. Gifts to donor-advised funds and private foundations don't qualify.

  • ?
    Itemizers: a 0.5%-of-AGI floor.

    From 2026, itemized charitable deductions are reduced by 0.5% of adjusted gross income. On $150,000 of AGI, the first $750 of gifts produces no deduction.

  • ?
    Annual ceilings.

    Cash gifts to public charities: up to 60% of AGI. Appreciated stock and property: 30% of AGI. Excess carries forward five years. Taxpayers in the 37% bracket get at most 35¢ of benefit per dollar.

  • ✓
    Charitable mileage stays at 14¢.

    Set by statute, not by the IRS, so it doesn't rise with the business rate.

  • ✕
    Not deductible at all.

    Gifts to individuals (including crowdfunding for a person), political campaigns, most foreign charities, the value of your time, raffle tickets, and dues or tuition that buy you something.

Record-keeping rules, in plain English

What you need on file for each kind of gift so the deduction survives an audit.

Cash, check, card, payroll

  • ✓
    Any amount

    A bank record (cancelled check, card or bank statement) or a written receipt from the charity showing name, date and amount. No record, no deduction.

  • ✓
    $250 or more (single gift)

    A contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the charity: amount, whether you received goods or services, and their value. Get it before you file.

  • ?
    You got something back

    Gala dinner, auction item, concert tickets: deduct only the amount above the value received. Over $75, the charity must tell you that value in writing.

  • ✓
    Payroll giving

    Pay stub or W-2 plus the pledge card naming the charity.

  • ✓
    Text-message gifts

    The phone bill showing the charity, date and amount is your record.

Goods & property

  • ✓
    Under $250

    A receipt from the charity (name, date, location, description) unless it's impractical, like a drop box. Keep your own list with condition and value.

  • ✓
    $250 to $500

    Written acknowledgment describing the property (it doesn't need to state a value) plus whether you received anything in return.

  • ✓
    $500 to $5,000

    All of the above, plus how and when you acquired the items and your cost. Form 8283 Section A.

  • ?
    Over $5,000 (single item or similar group)

    Qualified appraisal, Form 8283 Section B signed by appraiser and charity. Over $500,000, attach the appraisal itself.

  • ✓
    Stock held over a year

    Deduct full market value (average of high and low on the transfer date), no capital-gains tax. Held a year or less: deduction limited to cost basis.

  • ✕
    Worn-out clothing and household items

    Must be in good used condition or better. Otherwise, $0.

Volunteer expenses

  • ✓
    Keep receipts and a log

    Date, organization, purpose, and amount or miles for each item. A mileage log with odometer readings or map distances is fine.

  • ✓
    $250 or more for one event or trip

    Written acknowledgment from the charity describing the services you provided and whether you got anything in return.

  • ✕
    Anything reimbursed

    If the charity paid you back, there's nothing to deduct.

Before you give

  • ✓
    Confirm the charity is qualified

    Search it in the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search. Churches and governments qualify without being listed.

  • ✓
    Date matters

    A check counts when mailed; a card gift when charged; stock when it leaves your account. A December 31 gift belongs to this year even if the charity deposits it in January.

  • ✓
    Keep records at least 3 years

    From the date you file the return that claims them. Longer for carryforwards.

Sources: IRS Publication 526 (Charitable Contributions), Publication 561 (Determining the Value of Donated Property), Form 8283 instructions. General information only; not tax advice.
